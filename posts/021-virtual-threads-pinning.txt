Our payment service spends most of its life simply waiting — AML checks, provider calls, database roundtrips. It ran the classic thread-per-request model on a bounded Tomcat pool, which means that under load most of those threads were just sitting there blocked on IO doing absolutely nothing useful, while fresh requests piled up in a queue behind them waiting for a worker to free up. Java 21 virtual threads promised exactly the fix we wanted: keep the boring, readable, synchronous blocking style we already had, but let the JVM park a blocked thread for almost nothing instead of holding an expensive OS thread hostage for the entire duration of a slow external call.

So we flipped it on with high expectations… and one hot path actually got worse. The culprit was 𝗽𝗶𝗻𝗻𝗶𝗻𝗴. When a virtual thread enters a 𝘀𝘆𝗻𝗰𝗵𝗿𝗼𝗻𝗶𝘇𝗲𝗱 block (or drops into a native call), it gets pinned to its carrier OS thread and cannot unmount while it blocks inside that section. We had a 𝘀𝘆𝗻𝗰𝗵𝗿𝗼𝗻𝗶𝘇𝗲𝗱 guard around access to a shared client, so under real concurrency all of our shiny new virtual threads were quietly serializing onto a tiny handful of carrier threads. Replacing 𝘀𝘆𝗻𝗰𝗵𝗿𝗼𝗻𝗶𝘇𝗲𝗱 with a 𝗥𝗲𝗲𝗻𝘁𝗿𝗮𝗻𝘁𝗟𝗼𝗰𝗸 let them unmount properly while waiting, and only then did the latency numbers finally start moving in the right direction.

The bigger lesson landed right after that one. Virtual threads removed the thread bottleneck — but they do not magically remove the real one underneath it. Our database connection pool still held 30 connections, and the provider still rate-limited us exactly like before. So instead of requests queueing politely on the thread pool, we now had thousands of virtual threads all stampeding the same connection pool at the same instant. We had not deleted the bottleneck at all, we had simply shoved it one layer deeper and made it considerably less visible in our dashboards. Bounded downstream resources still need real backpressure, virtual threads or not.

What I actually walked away with: virtual threads are a genuine win for IO-bound, blocking code, and they let that plain synchronous style scale in a way it never could before. But they are not a "make everything faster" switch you casually flip in production. Watch carefully for pinning, never pool them (you create one per task — that is the entire point of them), and keep your bounded downstreams bounded on purpose 🙂
