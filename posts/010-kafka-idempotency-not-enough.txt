We had Kafka. We had idempotency. And we still charged users twice.

This happened in a payment flow running on Kubernetes with async processing via Apache Kafka. Everything looked “correct”: retries were in place, operations were idempotent, jobs were distributed across instances. But there was a tiny timing gap between picking an operation and updating its status — and that was enough. Two instances grabbed the same operation and both executed it. In fintech, that’s not just a bug — 𝗶𝘁’𝘀 𝗿𝗲𝗮𝗹 𝗺𝗼𝗻𝗲𝘆 𝗹𝗼𝘀𝘁.

The fix wasn’t adding yet another tool. It was accepting a simple idea: 𝗻𝗼 𝘀𝗶𝗻𝗴𝗹𝗲 𝗺𝗲𝗰𝗵𝗮𝗻𝗶𝘀𝗺 𝗴𝘂𝗮𝗿𝗮𝗻𝘁𝗲𝗲𝘀 𝗰𝗼𝗿𝗿𝗲𝗰𝘁𝗻𝗲𝘀𝘀 in distributed systems. We ended up layering protection — 𝗶𝗱𝗲𝗺𝗽𝗼𝘁𝗲𝗻𝗰𝘆 for safe retries, 𝗞𝗮𝗳𝗸𝗮 for decoupling, 𝗷𝗼𝗯-𝗹𝗲𝘃𝗲𝗹 𝗱𝗲𝗱𝘂𝗽𝗹𝗶𝗰𝗮𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻, and finally 𝗗𝗕 𝗿𝗼𝘄 𝗹𝗼𝗰𝗸𝗶𝗻𝗴 (SELECT FOR UPDATE) to guarantee that only one process can actually execute the operation. That last layer is usually underestimated — but it’s the one that closes the race.

𝗞𝗮𝗳𝗸𝗮 guarantees delivery. It does 𝗻𝗼𝘁 guarantee that your business logic runs exactly once. And 𝗶𝗱𝗲𝗺𝗽𝗼𝘁𝗲𝗻𝗰𝘆 helps, but it doesn’t eliminate every edge case when state transitions themselves are concurrent.

After that case, I stopped thinking in terms of “we use Kafka, we’re safe”. Now it’s always: 𝘄𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗵𝗮𝗽𝗽𝗲𝗻𝘀 𝗶𝗳 𝘁𝗵𝗶𝘀 𝗹𝗮𝘆𝗲𝗿 𝗳𝗮𝗶𝗹𝘀 — 𝗮𝗻𝗱 𝘄𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗰𝗮𝘁𝗰𝗵𝗲𝘀 𝗶𝘁?

Have you ever seen a system that was “theoretically safe” but still broke in production? 👇
